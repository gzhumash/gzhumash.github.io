Training Fundamentals

Knowing which exercise targets which muscle is only half of it. This page explains how to use those exercises over weeks and months so you keep getting stronger.

What Is Progressive Overload?

Progressive overload means gradually increasing the demands you place on your muscles over time. Your body adapts to whatever stress you give it. The first time you bench 95 pounds for 10 reps it is hard, but after a few weeks your muscles and nervous system adjust and the same weight feels easy.

Once that happens, repeating the exact same workout only maintains what you have. To keep building muscle and strength, the work has to keep getting a little harder over time.

The Training Variables

Training variables are the things you can change to make a workout harder or easier. Progressive overload is just increasing one of these over time.

  • Load: how much weight you lift.
  • Reps: how many times you do the movement in one set without stopping.
  • Sets: how many rounds of reps you do for an exercise.
  • Rest: how long you recover between sets.
  • Tempo: how fast you lift and lower the weight.
  • Frequency: how many times per week you train a muscle.
  • Range of motion: how far you move through each rep.
  • Exercise selection: which movements you choose, like going from push-ups to dips.

Reps and Sets by Goal

Different goals respond best to different combinations of the variables above. Use this table as a starting point.

General guidelines for each training goal
Goal Reps per set Sets per exercise Rest between sets Load (% of 1 rep max)
Strength 1 to 5 3 to 6 2 to 5 minutes 85 to 100%
Muscle growth 6 to 12 3 to 5 60 to 90 seconds 67 to 85%
Muscular endurance 12 to 20+ 2 to 3 30 to 60 seconds Under 67%

Ways to Progress

There is more than one way to make a workout harder:

  1. Add reps. Keep the same weight and do one or two more reps than last time.
  2. Add weight. Once you hit the top of your rep range on every set, increase the weight by the smallest amount you can.
  3. Add a set. Doing more total work increases volume without changing the weight.
  4. Slow down the tempo. Take 3 seconds to lower the weight instead of dropping it.
  5. Shorten your rest. Do the same work in less time.
  6. Increase range of motion. Go deeper on squats or stretch further on flyes, with good form.

Only change one variable at a time. If you add weight, reps, and sets all in the same week, you won't know what worked and you are more likely to get hurt.

A 4 Week Example

Here is what progressive overload can look like for one exercise using double progression: first add reps within a range, then add weight and start the range over.

Dumbbell Bench Presses, 3 sets of 8 to 12 reps
Week Weight (each hand) Sets x Reps Total volume What changed
1 50 lb 3 x 8 2,400 lb Starting point
2 50 lb 3 x 10 3,000 lb Added reps
3 50 lb 3 x 12 3,600 lb Hit the top of the range
4 55 lb 3 x 8 2,640 lb Added weight, reset reps

Total volume counts both dumbbells (weight x 2 x sets x reps). Week 4 has less volume than week 3, but the weight is heavier, so the cycle starts over at a higher level.

Common Mistakes

  • Adding weight too fast. If your form breaks down, the target muscle stops doing the work and your joints take the stress instead.
  • Switching exercises every week. You can't overload a movement you never repeat. Stick with the same exercises for at least 4 to 8 weeks.
  • Not tracking your workouts. If you don't write down what you lifted, you can't know whether you beat it.
  • Going to failure on every set. Leaving 1 to 2 reps in the tank on most sets helps you recover and train consistently.
  • Ignoring recovery. Muscles grow while you rest, so sleep, food, and rest days matter too.

Putting It Together

  1. Find your exercises and pick 1 to 2 for each muscle you want to train.
  2. Choose a goal from the table above and set your reps, sets, and rest.
  3. Pick a weight you can lift for the bottom of your rep range with good form.
  4. Write down every workout, and try to beat last week by one rep or a little weight.
  5. Every 4 to 8 weeks, take an easier week to let your body recover.
